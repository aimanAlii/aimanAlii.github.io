# Coding With a Machine That Talks Back: My First Month of AI Pair Programming

A few weeks ago I decided to stop treating AI coding assistants like fancy autocomplete and start actually *pairing* with one — the way you would with a human teammate. Here's what I learned.

## The Setup

Pair programming has always had a simple premise: two people, one keyboard, better code. One person drives, the other navigates — catching bugs, asking questions, thinking a step ahead. Swapping the navigator for an AI assistant sounded gimmicky at first. It turned out to be one of the more useful workflow changes I've made in years.

My setup was simple:
- An AI coding assistant wired into my editor
- A habit of narrating what I was doing, out loud or in comments
- A rule: no blindly accepting suggestions without understanding them

## What Actually Changed

**1. I stopped Googling as much.**
Instead of tabbing away to search "how to debounce a function in JavaScript," I could just ask, get an explanation *in the context of my actual code*, and keep moving. The AI wasn't just handing me a Stack Overflow answer — it was reasoning about my specific variable names, my project's conventions, my edge cases.

**2. Code reviews got easier before they even started.**
Explaining a tricky function to the AI to get its take often forced me to explain it clearly to *myself* first. Half the bugs I caught weren't caught by the AI — they were caught by me, mid-sentence, realizing my logic didn't hold up.

**3. Boilerplate stopped eating my afternoons.**
Config files, repetitive test scaffolding, CRUD endpoints — the unglamorous 30% of programming that eats a disproportionate amount of time. Handing that off freed up real hours for the parts of the work that actually needed my judgment.

**4. I got better at asking questions.**
This one surprised me. Working with an AI assistant made me sharper at articulating *what I actually wanted* — a skill that turned out to help with human collaborators too.

## Where It Fell Short

It wasn't all smooth. A few honest notes:

- **Context windows aren't infinite.** On a large, sprawling codebase, the AI sometimes lost track of decisions made a hundred files ago. I learned to keep it anchored with clear, current context rather than assuming it remembered everything.
- **Confidently wrong is still wrong.** A few times I got a clean, well-formatted answer that was subtly incorrect. Treating AI output as a first draft — not a final answer — saved me more than once.
- **It doesn't replace design thinking.** Architecture decisions, tradeoffs, "should this even be a microservice" conversations — those still need a human (or a team of humans) in the loop.

## The Real Takeaway

AI pair programming isn't about writing less code — it's about spending your attention differently. The tedious 60% gets compressed, and what's left is a bigger share of the interesting 40%: design decisions, edge cases, the actual *thinking* part of software engineering.

It's not a replacement for a human pair. It's a different tool with a different shape — less like a colleague, more like a very fast, very literal-minded rubber duck that occasionally writes the code for you.

Would I go back to coding without it? Probably not. Would I trust it blindly? Also no. That balance — leaning on it heavily while staying the one who understands the code — is, so far, the whole game.
